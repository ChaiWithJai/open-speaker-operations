# How to write an AI Engineer CFP proposal

A strong proposal makes a specific promise to a specific audience. It gives a human reviewer enough evidence to picture the session, assess its fit, and trust that the speaker can deliver it.

## Start with the audience outcome

Write one sentence in this form: “After this session, a **[level]** attendee who **[current situation]** can **[observable outcome]**.” Use that sentence to decide what belongs in the abstract and what does not.

## Pick a title that earns attention

Prefer a concrete tension, result, or surprising constraint. Avoid “Introduction to…”, unexplained product names, and claims such as “revolutionary.” A title may be edited later, so clarity matters more than cleverness at submission time.

## Write the abstract

Use four short moves:

1. Name the real problem and who experiences it.
2. Explain what the session will demonstrate or teach.
3. Give two or three concrete takeaways.
4. State the assumed experience level.

Reviewers need the proposed session, not the speaker biography. Put credentials and personal context in the speaker profile.

## Fit the AIE program

Choose one format and one to five topics. AIE welcomes beginners and future stars as well as established practitioners. Useful genres include a production case study, technical deep dive, live build, evaluation, incident lesson, research-to-practice translation, or an argued point of view backed by evidence.

Vendor-affiliated proposals cannot be vendor-only main-stage talks. A workshop, leadership session, expo session, or booth context may be appropriate when the commercial relationship is disclosed. Every proposal is reviewed by a person.

## Understand a selective program

At a 5–15% acceptance rate, competent proposals still lose to a clearer program fit or a more complementary program mix. Make the evidence easy to find: scope the claim, disclose constraints, say what is genuinely novel, and show what an attendee can apply afterward. Multiple submissions are allowed; send distinct, considered ideas rather than small title variations.

## What happens after submission

A human reviewer scores program fit, speaker value, and practicality, then recommends accept, discuss, or reject. The chair retains decision authority. Acceptances are issued in three waves—August 15, September 1, and September 15—followed by speaker onboarding, travel coordination, recording consent, and production tasks.
